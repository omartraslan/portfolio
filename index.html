The first 30 minutes

The first half hour of a major incident sets its direction. In that window I establish a clear shared understanding of the situation, define roles and accountability, and focus the team on mitigating customer impact before pursuing root cause. Communication starts at once, to internal and external stakeholders, on a fixed cadence, so everyone works from the same information while we manage against SLAs.

Evidence

  • Peak-load operations. I designed and led 12 monthly payroll hypercare bridges, each running 48 hours. They covered the bank’s most critical recurring processing window and brought infrastructure, core banking, online banking and mainframe teams together with senior directors.
  • Disaster recovery. I took part in 3 disaster recovery drills as a Major Incident Manager. In each one I ran the DR bridge on my shift, handed it over at every shift change, worked with the engineering teams and directors on the bridge and reported to the DR committee at the end of each shift. The largest was an 11-day drill that simulated a cybersecurity incident and required a full switchover to the alternate data center. It processed more than 1 billion transactions with no interruption to customer service.
  • Change control during incidents. I evaluated and implemented emergency changes and led 42 post-change window business-as-usual verifications, run with the Command Center agents’ weekly post-change jobs according to the change management schedule. I introduced a handoff for post-implementation verification between the incident and change management teams.
  • Command structure. I introduced a business-impact severity matrix based on the impact and urgency model, and co-developed the accountability boundaries between Unified Command Center teams with the Process Improvement Director.
  • Observability. I partnered with the SRE and observability team on 134 service journey mappings in Dynatrace, giving structured visibility across the bank’s critical services.

A hard day: an outage and an incident underneath it

At Marqeta, a cloud provider’s regional outage ran about 15 hours, affecting about 40 services, 11+ of them critical, and producing 26 incident tickets.

Organizing the response

  • Rapid swarm. I initiated a 10-minute rapid swarm with all service SMEs to enforce structure.
  • One source of truth. I consolidated the related tickets into a parent-child hierarchy, which eliminated information drift.
  • Role specialization. I separated the technical incident commander role from a communications lead role.
  • Stakeholder alignment. I activated the crisis management protocol to bridge engineering and executive leadership, and set up a dedicated communications bridge with the VP of Communications and Customer Success to centralize and vet external-facing information.
  • Executive visibility. A synchronized MI report every 30 minutes reduced executive inquiry volume.
  • Afterwards. I used the case to formalize a Crisis-Incident Handoff Playbook.

The incident underneath

Inside the outage, a second and unrelated failure was building: a card network update that our message router could not process.

  1. Declare and mobilize

    I was paged for sustained stand-in processing on a card network. I opened a bridge, requested responders including production support and the account managers, and put the engineers on a single facts document.

  2. Test the obvious cause

    The timing pointed at the outage, and early evidence agreed: an identity-check dependency was down. I linked the incident to the outage as related and kept recording the facts as they changed.

  3. Separate the incident

    By afternoon the dependency had recovered but the errors continued, and only one network was affected. I told colleagues and stakeholders it was a different cause, traced to the network’s update.

  4. Contain and verify

    The network would not roll back, so engineering shipped a containment fix. In parallel I ran a service-by-service recovery check for the outage and held status updates until the messages to top customers were agreed.

  5. Close out

    I chased every related incident to a stable state with its impact window, supported the customer reports, and tracked the follow-up actions.

What I took from it

  • Keep a light watch after dismissing a bridge, because a second failure can be hiding behind the first.
  • Close the impact question before asking a third party to change anything.
  • Reconcile report timelines with customers’ own data before publishing.

The same method elsewhere

  • NCR Global Command Center. I led P1/P2 bridges for global banking and enterprise clients with resolver teams of 8 to 12 engineers. One example is a weekend outage that took down 35 school sites, handled with limited on-shift resources: I escalated the resourcing limit to the service management office, agreed a dispatch plan with the network provider and shared a status list at the end of every shift.

In a new environment

Critical processing windows, resilience exercises and participant-facing incidents call for the same discipline: one commander, clear roles, customer impact mitigated first, and communication on a fixed cadence from the first minutes.

Executive and customer communication

During a major incident, executives and customers need the same facts, from one source, at a predictable time. I design the format, the cadence and the measures, so communication improves from one incident to the next.

Approach

Each update states the customer impact, the customer communications plan, the recovery estimate and whether leadership needs to act. Updates go out on schedule even when there is no new information, because a missed update creates more inbound questions than an unchanged status does.

Update format

Customer impact
Which customers are affected and their tiers, what those customers and their end users cannot do, and the financial effect
Customer communications
What customers have been told, through which channel, and when the next update is due
Recovery
Current estimate, confidence level, next update time
Leadership action
The decision or intervention needed, or “none at this time”
P1 Major Incident Update 3, 10:45 ET Illustrative, not real incident data
Customer impact
Cardholders at affected customers are seeing declined or delayed payments since 09:52. 14 customers are affected. Tier 1 customers are seeing failures mainly on in-store card payments. No data loss identified.
3 Tier 16 Tier 25 Tier 3
Customer communications
Status page updated at 10:05 and 10:40. Tier 1 customers have been notified directly by their account managers. Next customer update due by 11:20.
Recovery

Leadership action
None at this time. Approval of the Tier 1 customer notification wording is needed by 11:00.

What I measure

  • Time to first customer communication. Captured on every incident record by an agent that reads the incident and customer channels and writes the first notification time into Jira.
  • Communication within SLA. Tracked per Triage Coordinator in a monthly scorecard of 15 KPIs, reconciled across Jira, PagerDuty and Slack.

32% less time to first customer communication at Marqeta, quarter over quarter. I co-designed and deployed the Inci-Bot communications agent. It drafts the first customer communication for a production engineer or Major Incident Manager to review in about two minutes before publishing.

Automation and AI agents

I remove manual work from incident operations in steps: first visibility, then automation, then agents. A person stays accountable for anything a customer sees.

  1. Visibility: NCR

    Field engineers were being sent to sites that had already restored themselves, because restoration notices were buried in ticket logs. I built a ServiceNow dashboard matching active dispatches against restoration signals, added an automation that scanned tickets for those signals, and connected it to Slack so case owners were alerted to cancel the dispatch.

    Unnecessary dispatches fell 65% within three months.

  2. Integration: Al Rajhi Bank

    I integrated real-time P1 status SMS with ServiceNow, so the Executive Team had live updates while ServiceNow remained the single source of truth.

  3. Workflow automation: Marqeta

    Slack workflows for customer incident reports, status page updates and major incident reports, a maintenance notification workflow, a Triage Coordinator handover workflow and automated executive notifications. A PagerDuty workflow with Jira Automation fills the incident’s Slack channel on every incident ticket.

    40% of the incident team’s manual tasks automated.

  4. Agents and connected tools: Marqeta

    Inci-Bot, which I co-designed and deployed, reads Slack transcripts, Jira records and war room notes and produces a structured first draft of post-incident reviews and customer-facing incident reports. I tuned it with Legal and Technical Writing. Documentation turnaround fell from 5 to 2 business days.

    Communications agent. It drafts the first customer communication for a two-minute human review before publishing. Time to first customer communication fell 32% quarter over quarter.

    Notification agent. A Rovo agent with Jira Automation records when partners were first notified on every incident, now in its sixth release, with safeguards that distinguish a notification from a confirmation.

    Tool connections. I build MCP connections and workflow automation between the tools the incident team already uses, including Jira, Confluence, Slack and PagerDuty, so agents and skills can read and update them directly.

    Reusable AI skills for known error records, time-to-communicate analysis, the Triage Coordinator scorecard and monthly reporting to the executive team (ET).

Cross-functional leadership

I lead people I do not manage by agreeing roles, escalation paths and decision rights with them before an incident, so that during one the process runs on agreements already made.

How I build alignment

  1. Map the stakeholders

    Every team the change touches, rated by influence and by how much it affects them.

  2. Meet each one before proposing anything

    Learn how the current process affects their work, and where resistance will come from.

  3. Collect the bottlenecks

    Where handoffs fail, where ownership is unclear and where information goes missing.

  4. Draft, then refine with each team

    Every affected team shapes the framework before it is final.

  5. Present with backing and a sponsor

    Approval confirms an agreement, tied to a goal the sponsor owns.

  6. Embed it

    Train the people who run it, set an update cadence and review how it performs.

Where I used it

  • NCR runbook alignment. NOC, engineering and escalation teams used different runbooks. Following these steps removed ownership ambiguity during P1 incidents and made escalation faster and more decisive.
  • NCR after-hours change approval. Major Incident Managers were asked to act as change manager outside business hours. I worked individually with the NOC supervisor, the Quality and Process team and management, and agreed training, engagement conditions for the on-call change manager, and priority for the team in future change management roles.
  • Marqeta maintenance process. After feedback from engineering teams, I introduced a standard maintenance notification process: announcement and completion workflows, a dedicated channel, and guidelines that define the roles of the maintenance owner, the service owner and the incident manager. High-risk maintenance needs at least 48 hours’ notice and low-risk at least 24, with rollback as the default if maintenance causes impact. It improved cross-team awareness and gave incidents caused by maintenance a clear owner.
  • Marqeta training as an objective. Presenting incident training as a fourth-quarter organizational objective secured leadership backing and protected time: 384 engineers trained, 79% completion in one quarter, then added to onboarding.

Developing people

  • NCR. A 3-day ServiceNow and ITIL intensive for new hires, recorded for onboarding. Two participants moved into junior ITSM analyst roles.
  • Al Rajhi Bank. Recorded training for Command Center agents on incident practices and communication with Major Incident Managers.
  • Marqeta. Trained and onboarded the 24/7 incident triage team and published its handover process.

In a new environment

I would spend the first weeks in one-on-one conversations with the technology, risk, communications, operations and problem management leads before proposing any change to how incidents are run.

Customer advocacy

I treat the customer’s experience of an incident as part of the incident: what they are told, how soon, and whether it still holds when they check it against their own data.

White Glove at Marqeta

White Glove is Marqeta’s initiative for its largest accounts. It adds proactive, high-touch support on top of normal service: detection and change-control guardrails around the journeys those accounts depend on, a customer-facing program health dashboard, incident impact reports written for account teams, a tracked delivery path for each account’s product requests, and clear ownership of the systems that decide what customers hear and when.

My part is the incident and communication side of that promise, run as a weekly rhythm with the teams who own Tier 1 relationships: how customers hear about planned work, how quickly they hear about incidents, and whether the report they receive afterwards is one they can rely on.

A weekly rhythm with the Tier 1 teams

For Tier 1 accounts, incident management was a standing conversation, not a series of one-off messages. Each week we met with the teams who own those relationships, and I used the meeting to do three things:

  • Review the major incident SLAs. I presented the major incident SLAs each week and collected feedback on how incidents had been handled, specifically to spot customer pain early: updates that were late, unclear or missing, and gaps in how the process behaved for a particular account.
  • Align customer-facing incident reports. Reports were reviewed in the same forum, so the timeline of preventive actions in each report matched what engineering had committed to. Customers heard dates the teams could keep.
  • Align on scheduled events and maintenance. We reviewed upcoming events and maintenance that could affect a Tier 1 customer’s business, so the right notice and communication went out before the work, not after.

The weekly loop

  1. Present

    The major incident SLAs and the week’s incidents, in numbers.

  2. Collect

    Feedback from the people closest to each customer on where handling fell short.

  3. Fix

    The process or communication step behind each pain point.

  4. Report back

    The change at the next meeting, so the people who raised an issue see it closed.

What changed for Tier 1 customers

  • Fewer surprises. Planned work was announced in advance, with at least 48 hours’ notice for high-risk or customer-impacting maintenance.
  • Faster first contact. Time to first customer communication fell 32% quarter over quarter.
  • Commitments they can hold us to. Incident reports carried preventive action timelines that matched engineering’s plans.
  • A short path for complaints. Customer pain raised through the account teams reached the incident process within the week, and the fix was reported back.

Together these raised Tier 1 customers’ satisfaction with how incidents and planned work were handled: they heard sooner, heard the same account their account team heard, and had dates they could hold us to.

What I built for customers

  • A standard for planned maintenance. Maintenance is a common source of surprise for customers. I introduced a standard notification process: workflows to announce and close out each maintenance, notice of at least 48 hours for high-risk or customer-impacting work and 24 hours otherwise, a single named owner, and a defined path to an incident if maintenance causes impact. The White Glove workstream identified a customer-facing maintenance communication standard, covering notice periods, contractual windows and required content, as an open item, and this process is the foundation for it.
  • Faster first contact. I measure time to first customer communication on every incident and co-designed the communications agent that drafts the first message for a two-minute human review. First-contact time fell 32% quarter over quarter.
  • Reports customers can rely on. Every SEV0 and SEV1 incident gets a customer-facing incident report, reviewed by Legal before release. I coordinate the draft, the technical edit and the approval, and put the customer with the largest impact first, leaving wider detail to the post-mortem.
  • Learning that reaches engineers. I host a monthly incident roundup for engineering, so lessons from customer-impacting incidents reach the teams that can act on them.
  • Follow-through. I track post-mortem corrective actions to completion, because a customer’s trust depends on whether the fix happens.

A lesson from a customer. After a major incident, a customer compared our incident report with their own transaction data and found that its timestamps did not match what they had observed. That is the standard customers hold us to. It changed how I prepare reports: reconcile the timeline with the customer’s data first, publish second.

How I advocate

  • Tell customers first, and tell them plainly.
  • Put the most affected customer first.
  • Give every audience the same account.
  • Reconcile with the customer’s own data.
  • Close the loop with the fix.

In a new environment

I would agree notice periods and communication standards with the largest customers before an incident, then measure them on every incident.

First 90 days in a new role

A starting proposal, based on how I have introduced change in three environments. It assumes nothing about an organization’s current practices and would be adjusted after the first weeks.

Crawl

Days 1 to 30: learn and stabilize

  • Join live incidents to see how escalation works today.
  • One-on-ones with technology, risk, communications, operations and problem management leads.
  • Review recent major incident reports, open action items, severity definitions and on-call coverage.
  • Output: a current-state assessment and stakeholder map.

Walk

Days 31 to 60: pilot one improvement

  • Agree one improvement with the leader of the function, such as the customer update cadence or action item tracking.
  • Pilot it with one team and measure it against a baseline.
  • Output: pilot results and a recommendation to scale, change or stop.

Run

Days 61 to 90: scale with ownership

  • Scale what worked, with named owners and training for the incident management team.
  • Report the first KPI review to the leader of the function.
  • Output: KPI baseline, roadmap for the next two quarters.

Measures I would baseline

  • Major incident trends, quarter over quarter
  • Mean time to resolve, by severity
  • Mean time to failure, per service
  • Time to first customer communication
  • Post-incident action item closure rate
  • Repeat incident rate for the same root cause
